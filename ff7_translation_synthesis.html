    How the English FF7 Became a Different Game

    A synthesis of 636 translation differences across all 43 scenes of Final Fantasy VII

    Japanese original English localization Scene links go to the full entry.

    The Core Shift

    The Japanese FF7 is a game about vulnerable people who can barely articulate their feelings, caught in systems they didn't build, trying to save a world they can physically feel dying. The English FF7 is a game about cool, tough characters who fight a corporation to save the planet. Both versions tell the same story. They do not tell it in the same emotional register, and the cumulative effect of hundreds of small shifts is a substantially different experience of that story's themes, characters, and meaning.

    This analysis is based on a scene-by-scene comparison of the original Japanese script files against the official 1997 English localization, covering 636 individual translation differences. The translation was done under extreme time pressure by a small team, and most individual changes are understandable in isolation. What matters is the aggregate — the systematic directions in which the changes push.

    Characters: Who You Spend 60 Hours With

    Cloud — JP: Guarded Professional / EN: Edgy Loner

    The English Cloud is hostile where the Japanese Cloud is indifferent. When he rejects Barret's cause, the Japanese uses a polite hedge — JP 「悪いけど興味がないな」 "Sorry, but I'm not interested" — while the English gives us EN "It's not my problem." Scene 1 ›11 When he introduces himself to Aeris, the Japanese Cloud stutters: JP 「仕事は…… 仕事は「なんでも屋」だ」 "My job is… my job is a 'handyman.'" — repeating "my job is" twice, putting the label in quotation marks as if trying it on. The English breezes past: EN "I do a little bit of everything." Scene 4 ›6

    These aren't isolated cases. Across the full script, the Japanese Cloud is a man performing detachment as a coping mechanism, with vulnerability constantly leaking through — in stammers, hedges, self-directed bewilderment, and moments of absurd deadpan humor. In Japanese, he declares with solemn formality that JP it is his "principle" (主義) not to sit on trains; the English replaces this with a casual quirk: EN "I have a thing against sitting." Scene 3 ›5 In Japanese, he announces with inexplicable certainty that JP "There is something I need for cross-dressing in here. I know it."; the English replaces this with bland plot logic: EN "Looks like I need to go inside to complete my disguise." Scene 5 ›3 The English Cloud is simply cold, and the player's job is to be surprised when he eventually shows feeling. The Japanese Cloud's feelings are visible from the first scene; the player's job is to watch him learn to stop hiding them.

    The distinction becomes critical in the game's psychological climax. When Cloud's identity fractures, the Japanese marks it linguistically — he shifts into keigo (formal/polite speech), addressing Tifa as "Tifa-san," using verb endings that signal dissociation. Scene 31 ›9 English has no grammatical mechanism for this. When he tries to reclaim himself, his Japanese declaration is JP 「俺は俺の現実を生きる」 "I will live my reality" — an assertion of ownership. The English reframes this: EN "I'm going to live my life without pretending" — identity as the absence of fakery rather than something actively claimed. Scene 35 ›13

    Most damaging: in the final scene, the Japanese Cloud says he'll fight alongside his party — JP "I'll fight here too" — but the English renders this as EN "OK, I'm going alone", making Barret's response incoherent and converting the climactic moment from collective resolve to lone-wolf posturing. Scene 43 ›10

    Barret — JP: Rough Poet / EN: Angry Caricature

    Barret is the most systematically distorted character in the English script. The Japanese Barret clicks his tongue (チッ) and grunts dismissively (ケッ); the English invents cartoon profanity where none exists: EN "(*^%!" Scene 1 ›10 The Japanese Barret reaches for poetic imagery — JP a "golden thread of hope" (金の糸) Scene 7 ›12, Cloud is JP "dripping with lingering attachment" to Shinra (未練タラタラ) Scene 2 ›9 — while the English flattens these to EN "wire" and EN "misses." The Japanese Barret has moments of genuine moral self-examination: in Japanese he catches himself shifting from JP "we" to "I" when confessing guilt about Corel, arriving at individual accountability. The English keeps EN "we" throughout, erasing the distinction. Scene 20 ›12

    His most devastating line in Japanese — JP 「つぐないきれないさ」 "I can never fully atone for this" — becomes EN "I can't keep up with this", converting a moral reckoning into logistical overwhelm. Scene 34 ›14 His most politically incisive observation in Japanese — that Midgar might be "the place where all of humanity's ingenuity has ended up" — is collapsed in English into a flat comparison: EN "the total opposite of this place." Scene 30 ›6 The English also adds material with no Japanese basis: EN "lyin' white-faced…" is an appearance-based slur invented by the localization. Scene 22 ›5

    The aggregate effect is that the English Barret reads as a man who swears and yells — the Japanese Barret is rough-spoken but capable of lyricism, moral complexity, and surprising tenderness. The English localization inadvertently stripped out exactly the qualities that made him a three-dimensional character.

    Aeris — JP: Confident and Fate-Aware / EN: Sweet and Tentative

    The Japanese Aeris negotiates the price of her bodyguard with two blunt words: JP 「デート、１回！」 "One date!" The English hedges: EN "How about if I go out with you once?" Scene 4 ›3 When she teases Cloud, the Japanese uses the warm diminutive JP "CLOUD-chan" — genuinely affectionate, the suffix used for young girls and close friends. The English EN "Miss CLOUD" is ironic and distancing. Scene 5 ›5 When she assesses Cloud, the Japanese gives her melancholic insight: JP 「不幸な青年」 "an unhappy young man." The English reduces this to a diagnosis: EN "mixed-up kid." Scene 26 ›3

    Most critically, the Japanese Aeris carries a vocabulary of fate. She describes the Promised Land as JP 「運命の土地」 "land of destiny" Scene 25 ›10 — a word the English drops entirely. Her awareness of what's coming is conveyed through register shifts and fatalistic particles that English can't access. The cumulative loss: the English Aeris reads as a sweet girl who dies tragically, while the Japanese Aeris reads as a woman who understands more than she lets on and walks toward her fate with conscious resolve. In Japanese, you lose someone who was fully present. In English, death happens to her.

    Sephiroth — JP: Disturbingly Specific / EN: Conventionally Menacing

    The Japanese Sephiroth is scarier because his psychology is detailed. His laugh changes at the exact moment of his psychological break — from normal laughter to a darker, more sinister クックックッ — a sonic storytelling device the English collapses into identical EN "Ha ha ha"s throughout. Scene 10 ›8 When he conceives his plan, the Japanese uses startlingly childlike register: JP 「いいこと思いついた」 "I've thought of something good" — the phrasing of a child announcing an idea. The English gives us EN "great idea" — corporate brainstorming. Scene 10 ›9

    His most chilling line comes after killing Aeris: JP 「私の寄り道はもう終わった」 "My detour is now finished." He dismisses the central traumatic event of the game as an errand. This line is cut entirely from the English. Scene 29 ›1 In the same scene, the Japanese JP "do not grieve" (悲しむことはない) becomes EN "do not fear" — grief and fear are different targets, and the Japanese version is more insidious: he's consoling the party for a loss before they've even registered it. Scene 28 ›8

    When Cloud breaks down in the Temple of the Ancients and produces Sephiroth's exact phonetic laugh (クックックッ), the English renders this as generic EN "Ha ha ha", missing entirely that this is Sephiroth's voice coming out of Cloud's mouth. Scene 28 ›11

    His late-game self-description encapsulates the difference. The Japanese JP 「この私から」 "from this very me, everything begins" is a solipsistic origin-claim — he believes he is the source of reality. The English EN "with me" makes it mere self-inclusion. Scene 43 ›7

    The Supporting Cast

    Cait Sith suffers the most complete erasure. His Kansai/Osaka dialect — ほな, せや, やけど, おるんですわ — runs through every scene he appears in and immediately codes him as a specific Japanese archetype: the Osaka merchant, commercially minded, comedically unreliable, working-class. This is directly relevant to his narrative role as a corporate spy wearing a cheerful persona. English readers get none of this sociological shorthand — he just sounds generically colloquial.

    Red XIII undergoes an invisible characterization arc through Japanese pronouns. He initially presents himself with JP ワシ (washi) — an old man's pronoun, projecting wisdom and age. But when his guard drops, he slips into JP オイラ (oira) — a childish, rural pronoun that reveals he's actually young and immature for his species. Scene 20 ›2 At Cosmo Canyon, he announces his homecoming in the third person — JP 「ナナキ、帰りました」 "Nanaki has returned" — the way a small child talks in Japanese. English has only "I," so the entire arc is invisible: in Japanese, you can hear Red XIII performing maturity and failing, his pronouns betraying his youth long before the story confirms it.

    Cid is theatrical and idiom-rich in Japanese — ore-sama (grandiose self-reference), folk rhyming expressions, working-class metaphors about eating rice and abacus calculations. He asks half-questions rather than making declarations: JP 「俺たちじゃないのか、たぶん……」 "Isn't that us, perhaps?" The English makes him declarative: EN "Ya follow me? That someone is us." Scene 38 ›6 His moment of transcendent grief at the rocket launch — JP 「ンガーーーー！！」, pure inarticulate anguish beyond language — becomes EN "SH-------#%!!", domesticating a scream into a censored swear. Scene 23 ›9

    Tifa is more resolute in Japanese and more tentative in English. Her key philosophical observation in the Lifestream — JP that humans are able to forget — is reversed in English to EN "things they can never forget", a direct inversion of a thematically central idea. The Japanese Tifa is making a bittersweet point about the mercy of forgetting; the English Tifa is saying the opposite. Scene 35 ›12

    Vincent's self-correction mid-sentence — shifting from JP 愛する (love) to JP 尊けいする (respect), audibly catching himself — is one of the most character-defining moments in the game. The English lists both words as a pair, erasing the psychological tell of a man who can't bring himself to say "love" without flinching. Scene 22 ›7 In Japanese, both he and Lucrecia use the same word — JP 罪 (tsumi, sin/crime) — for their shared guilt. The English translates Lucrecia's correctly but renders Vincent's as EN "punishment", breaking the symmetry and converting mutual culpability into passive victimhood.

    Shera's Japanese framing is JP つぐなわなくては "I must make atonement" — an ethical debt term. The English becomes EN "I'll live my life for him" — converting guilt-driven penance into romantic devotion. Her entire character arc shifts from obligation to love. Scene 23 ›5

    Themes: What the Game Is About

    The Planet as Body vs. The Planet as Cause

    The Japanese uses specific, visceral, biological language for the Planet's suffering. In Japanese, land near reactors is JP 痩せていく "getting thinner" — emaciated like a starving person Scene 9 ›5; the planet's end is approaching JP ジワジワ — creepingly, inexorably, like a spreading stain Scene 4 ›15; Red XIII's vow uses the plant-dying verb JP 枯れさせる Scene 43 ›12; Barret speaks of JP 星の命 "the life of the Planet" rather than just "the Planet." Scene 2 ›8

    The English repeatedly abstracts this into generic environmentalism or exhaustion idioms (EN "on its last legs", EN "sucked dry"). The Japanese makes the environmental case viscerally — you feel the Planet as a body being damaged. The English makes it intellectually — you understand the Planet is in trouble. For a game whose central thesis is that the Planet is alive, this is a significant weakening.

    Shinra as Bureaucracy vs. Shinra as Villainy

    The Japanese Shinra commits evil through corporate process. In Japanese, mass murder requires authorization from JP "the Shinra board" (a collective decision); the English reduces this to EN "a Shinra Executive" (an individual). Scene 6 ›9 The Japanese identifies the act of calling the Planet's life "Mako energy" as itself the central deception — linguistic laundering. Scene 20 ›7 Rufus's chilling JP 「失敗は１度までだ」 "failure is permitted once" is corporate policy, stated as policy; the English renders it as a standard boss warning. Scene 16 ›7 The Confidential Report's most disturbing clause in Japanese — "there is no problem in leaving him abandoned as-is" — is cut entirely from the English. Scene 21 ›14

    Most subtly, a Midgar NPC uses the Japanese benefactive form (-てくれる) when describing what Shinra has "done for" the city — encoding ideological capture in grammar itself. The result: the Japanese Shinra is a recognizable corporation that does monstrous things through mundane procedures. The English Shinra is more conventionally villainous. The Japanese is more politically pointed precisely because it's more banal.

    Vulnerability vs. Cool

    This may be the single largest systematic shift. Across every character and every scene, the English localization:

    Converts vulnerability to hostility or indifference. Cloud's polite Japanese hedges (JP "Sorry, but...") become curt English dismissals (EN "It's not my problem"). Barret's Japanese moral reckonings (JP "I can never fully atone") become English logistical complaints (EN "I can't keep up with this"). Aeris's Japanese directness (JP "One date!") becomes English tentativeness (EN "How about if I go out with you once?").

    Completes unfinished sentences. Japanese characters trail off, stutter, repeat themselves, leave thoughts grammatically broken. The emotional weight of not being able to finish the sentence — Barret's stutter in Japanese before saying "pay" after Marlene gives him a flower Scene 2 ›15; Gast's stammering his family's names; Vincent's mid-sentence self-correction from "love" to "respect" Scene 22 ›7 — is consistently resolved into fluent English. The inarticulate becomes articulate, and something is lost every time.

    Resolves ambiguity into clarity. Cloud's Japanese breakdown speech leaves open whether his fear is genuinely his or Sephiroth's programming; the English resolves it. Aeris's Japanese JP 「初めて好きになった人」 "the first person I came to like" becomes EN "my first boyfriend", pre-answering Cloud's follow-up question and eliminating the bittersweet ambiguity about Zack. Scene 4 ›10

    Replaces somatic emotion with cognitive emotion. The Japanese JP 「目の奥が熱い」 "the back of my eyes is hot" — the physical precursor to tears — becomes EN "my eyes are burning" (irritation, not grief). Scene 29 ›4 Johnny's JP 「文学的になっちゃう」 "I somehow become literary" becomes EN "I get so mental." Scene 5 ›2 The body is replaced by the mind as the site of feeling.

    The aggregate: the Japanese FF7 is a game about people who are bad at expressing their emotions, played in a language whose grammar lets you hear them failing. The English FF7 is a game about people who are good at suppressing their emotions, played in a language that makes them sound competent even when they're falling apart.

    Fate and Agency

    Japanese characters accept fate more readily and explicitly. Cloud's Japanese rail metaphor specifies JP "rails that have been laid" (external force, passive voice) Scene 2 ›3; in Japanese he describes himself as JP "being summoned" (呼ばれていた) by Sephiroth, while the English frames it as EN actively chasing him; characters say JP しかたない ("it can't be helped") where the English gives active alternatives like EN "OK, let's go"; Aeris uses the word JP 運命 (destiny/fate) where the English drops it entirely.

    The English tendency to convert passive acceptance into active choice sounds like an improvement, but it undermines the tragedy. When characters have more agency, their failures become their fault. When they're caught in systems and fate, their struggles are more sympathetic and their defiance more meaningful. The Japanese game earns its emotional weight partly through this fatalism — these people aren't heroes who chose wrong; they're people trying to act within constraints they can barely see.

    The Comedy Gap

    The Japanese humor is character-revealing. In Japanese, Mukki invokes JP "the grime and sweat of our youth" with grandiose ceremony for a forced communal bath Scene 5 ›10; the Honey Bee Inn soldiers whine in childish grammar about their armor Scene 5 ›15; Jessie casts her bomb's first explosion as a JP 「晴れぶたい」 "debut performance" Scene 2 ›14; the vending machine products have absurd 1990s Japanese health-product branding. Scene 5 ›13 The comedy tells you who people are.

    The English humor tends toward broader strokes: invented insults (EN "I've had better dog food" where the Japanese Cloud primly says "it didn't suit my palate" Scene 5 ›14), American cultural references (a Gettysburg Address parody in Wutai), and randomness. It's often funny — sometimes funnier than the original — but it's less characterologically informative.

    The biggest structural comedy loss is repetition-as-joke. The Chocobo Sage uses JP the exact same phrase every time his memory "returns" — the mechanical repetition is the joke. The English EN varies the wording each iteration, trying to avoid monotony, and kills the gag's architecture. Similarly, Corneo's signature vocalization JP ほひ～ Scene 5 ›7 maintains grotesque coherence across all his emotional states in Japanese; the English rotates different approximations, undermining the characterization through phonetics that made him distinctively repulsive.

    The Error Layer

    Beyond systematic shifts, approximately 30 lines across the 43 scenes say something materially different from or opposite to the Japanese:

    • Reno's self-contradicting order in Japanese (JP "Fire! — Don't fire!") becomes a straight command in English (EN "Attack! Attack! Attack!"), removing both the comedy and the acknowledgment that killing Aeris would be catastrophic. Scene 4 ›4
    • Tifa's Japanese observation that humans are able to forget is reversed in English to EN "things they can never forget." Scene 35 ›12
    • Cloud's Japanese solidarity (JP "I'll fight here too") becomes English isolation (EN "I'm going alone"). Scene 43 ›10
    • Barret's Japanese JP "I can never fully atone" becomes EN "I can't keep up with this." Scene 34 ›14
    • Bugenhagen's Japanese self-criticism JP "Am I accepting fate too readily?" becomes the opposite in English: EN "Am I wishing against fate?" Scene 20 ›8
    • Cloud's Japanese motive for saving Barret — the darkly comic JP "I'd have bad dreams" — is replaced in English with EN "We need your help to save the Planet", accidentally making Cloud noble where he was self-interestedly funny. Scene 18 ›4
    • Marlene's cheerful Japanese JP 「あのね！」 "Um, listen!" becomes the distressed English EN "Help, I'm—", implying she's being harmed when she's actually excited. Scene 26 ›15
    • Ifalna's Japanese JP "Right now, I am very happy" becomes EN "I feel so much better now", converting a moment of pure joy into comparative recovery, making what follows less tragic. Scene 30 ›15

    These aren't systematic — they go in various directions — but they confirm that the translation was done under conditions where careful cross-checking wasn't possible.

    What the English Player Got

    The English FF7 is a great game. Its rougher translation became part of its identity — "This guy are sick" and "Let's mosey" are beloved precisely because they're odd. Some English inventions are genuinely superior (the Rude/crude pun; EN "I've had better dog food" is funnier than the Japanese original's prim "it didn't suit my palate").

    But the English player experienced a game that was emotionally cooler, thematically vaguer, and characterologically simpler than what the Japanese developers wrote. The environmental argument was more abstract. The corporate critique was more generic. The characters were tougher and less vulnerable. The humor was broader. The fate-vs-agency tension was muted. The grief was more punctuated and less sustained.

    FF7 is praised in the West for being the JRPG that "grew up" — the one that dealt with death, identity, and corporate evil in ways that felt adult. Bilingual fans and fan translators have pointed out individual discrepancies for decades. But seeing 636 of them together reveals something the individual comparisons don't: the shifts aren't random. They push consistently in the same directions — toward coolness, clarity, and toughness; away from vulnerability, ambiguity, and the specific textures of Japanese emotional expression. The translation didn't ruin the game — the story is too structurally strong for that — but it consistently filed down exactly the edges that made the original a more psychologically complex, more politically pointed work than most English-speaking players ever got to experience.
