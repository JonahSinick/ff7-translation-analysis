# How the English FF7 Became a Different Game

A synthesis of 636 translation differences across all 43 scenes of Final Fantasy VII's script.

## The Core Shift

The Japanese FF7 is a game about vulnerable people who can barely articulate their feelings, caught in systems they didn't build, trying to save a world they can physically feel dying. The English FF7 is a game about cool, tough characters who fight a corporation to save the planet. Both versions tell the same story. They do not tell it in the same emotional register, and the cumulative effect of hundreds of small shifts is a substantially different experience of that story's themes, characters, and meaning.

The translation was done under extreme time pressure by a small team, and most individual changes are understandable in isolation. What matters is the aggregate — the systematic directions in which the changes push.

## Characters: Who You Spend 60 Hours With

### Cloud: Apathetic Professional → Edgy Loner

The English Cloud is hostile where the Japanese Cloud is indifferent. When he rejects Barret's cause, the Japanese uses a polite hedge — 「悪いけど興味がないな」, "Sorry, but I'm not interested" — while the English gives us "It's not my problem." When he introduces himself to Aeris, he stutters: 「仕事は…… 仕事は「なんでも屋」だ」 — repeating "my job is" twice, putting the label in quotation marks as if trying it on. The English breezes past: "I do a little bit of everything."

These aren't isolated cases. Across the full script, Japanese Cloud is a man *performing* detachment as a coping mechanism, with vulnerability constantly leaking through the cracks — in stammers, hedges, self-directed bewilderment, and moments of absurd deadpan humor (his solemn "principle" about not sitting on trains; his inexplicable certainty that something he needs for cross-dressing is inside the Honey Bee Inn). English Cloud is simply cold, and the player's job is to be surprised when he eventually shows feeling. Japanese Cloud's feelings are visible from the first scene; the player's job is to watch him learn to stop hiding them.

The distinction becomes critical in the game's psychological climax. When Cloud's identity fractures, the Japanese marks it *linguistically* — he shifts into keigo (formal/polite speech), addressing Tifa as "Tifa-san," using verb endings that signal dissociation. English has no grammatical mechanism for this. When he tries to reclaim himself, his Japanese declaration is 「俺は俺の現実を生きる」 — "I will live my reality" — an assertion of ownership. The English "I'm going to live my life without pretending" reframes identity as the absence of fakery rather than something actively claimed.

Most damaging: in the final scene, Cloud's Japanese line means "I'll fight here too" — solidarity with his party. The English renders this as "OK, I'm going alone," making Barret's response incoherent and converting the climactic moment from collective resolve to lone-wolf posturing.

### Barret: Rough Poet → Angry Stereotype

Barret is the most systematically distorted character in the English script, and the pattern has uncomfortable implications. The Japanese Barret clicks his tongue (チッ) and grunts dismissively (ケッ); the English invents cartoon profanity ("(*^%!"). The Japanese Barret reaches for poetic imagery — a "golden thread of hope" (金の糸), the observation that Cloud is "dripping with lingering attachment" to Shinra (未練タラタラ) — while the English flattens these to "wire" and "misses." The Japanese Barret has moments of genuine moral self-examination: he catches himself shifting from "we" to "I" when confessing guilt about Corel, arriving at individual accountability. The English keeps "we," erasing the distinction.

His most devastating line — 「つぐないきれないさ」, "I can never fully atone for this" — becomes "I can't keep up with this," converting a moral reckoning into logistical overwhelm. His most politically incisive observation — that Midgar might be "the place where all of humanity's ingenuity has ended up" — is collapsed into a flat comparison ("the total opposite of this place"). And the English adds material with no Japanese basis: "lyin' white-faced…" is an appearance-based slur invented by the localization.

The aggregate effect is that English Barret reads as the angry Black man with a gun who swears and yells — a racial stereotype the Japanese character was never written as. Japanese Barret is rough-spoken but capable of lyricism, moral complexity, and surprising tenderness. He is the character the localization most betrayed.

### Aeris: Confident and Fate-Aware → Sweet and Passive

The Japanese Aeris negotiates the price of her bodyguard with two blunt words: 「デート、１回！」 — "One date!" The English hedges: "How about if I go out with you once?" When she teases Cloud, she calls him "CLOUD-chan" — a warm diminutive for young girls, genuinely affectionate. The English "Miss CLOUD" is ironic and distancing. When she assesses Cloud, she calls him 「不幸な青年」 — "an unhappy young man" — with melancholic insight. The English gives us "mixed-up kid."

Most critically, the Japanese Aeris carries a vocabulary of fate. She describes the Promised Land as 「運命の土地」 — "land of destiny." Her relationship to her Materia is an active emotional position: "It's fine even if it doesn't serve a purpose." Her awareness of what's coming is conveyed through register shifts and fatalistic particles that the English can't access. The cumulative loss is that English Aeris reads as a sweet girl who dies tragically, while Japanese Aeris reads as a woman who understands more than she lets on and walks toward her fate with conscious resolve. The tragedy is different: in Japanese, you lose someone who was fully present. In English, death happens *to* her rather than being something she faces.

### Sephiroth: Psychologically Unstable → Generically Powerful

The Japanese Sephiroth is scarier because he's unpredictable. His laugh changes at the exact moment of his psychological break — from normal laughter to a darker クックックッ — a sonic storytelling device the English collapses into identical "Ha ha ha"s. When he conceives his plan, he uses startlingly childlike register: "I've thought of something good" (いいこと思いついた). English gives us corporate brainstorming: "great idea."

His most chilling line after killing Aeris — 「私の寄り道はもう終わった」, "My detour is now finished" — is cut entirely from the English. He dismisses the central traumatic event of the game as an errand. His "do not grieve" (悲しむことはない) becomes "do not fear" — grief and fear are different targets, and the Japanese version is more insidious because he's consoling the party for a loss before they've even registered it.

When Cloud breaks down in the Temple of the Ancients and produces Sephiroth's exact phonetic laugh (クックックッ), the English renders this as generic "Ha ha ha," missing entirely that this is *Sephiroth's voice coming out of Cloud's mouth* — one of the most disturbing moments in the game, reduced to nothing.

His late-game self-description — 「この私から」, "from this very me, everything begins" — is a solipsistic origin-claim. The English "with me" makes it mere self-inclusion. The Japanese Sephiroth believes he is the source of reality. The English one believes he's important.

### The Supporting Cast

**Cait Sith** suffers the most complete erasure. His Kansai/Osaka dialect — ほな, せや, やけど, おるんですわ — runs through every scene he appears in and immediately codes him as a specific Japanese archetype: the Osaka merchant, commercially minded, comedically unreliable, working-class. This is directly relevant to his narrative role as a corporate spy wearing a cheerful persona. English readers get none of this sociological shorthand. He just sounds generically colloquial.

**Cid** is theatrical and idiom-rich in Japanese — ore-sama (grandiose self-reference), folk rhyming expressions, working-class metaphors about eating rice and abacus calculations. He asks half-questions rather than making declarations: 「俺たちじゃないのか、たぶん……」 — "Isn't that us, perhaps?" The English makes him a confident blowhard: "That someone is us." His moment of transcendent grief at the rocket launch — 「ンガーーーー！！」, pure inarticulate anguish beyond language — is domesticated to a censored swear.

**Tifa** is more resolute in Japanese and more tentative in English. Her key philosophical observation in the Lifestream — that humans are able to *forget* — is reversed to "things they can never forget," a direct inversion of a thematically central idea.

**Vincent's** self-correction mid-sentence — shifting from 愛する (love) to 尊けいする (respect), audibly catching himself — is one of the most character-defining moments in the game. The English lists both words as a pair, erasing the psychological tell. His sin/crime (罪) symmetry with Lucrecia is broken by translating his as "punishment," converting mutual culpability into passive victimhood.

**Shera's** "atonement" (つぐなわなくては, an ethical debt term) becomes "I'll live my life for him" — converting guilt-driven penance into romantic devotion. Her entire character arc shifts from obligation to love.

## Themes: What the Game Is About

### The Planet as Body vs. The Planet as Cause

The Japanese uses specific, visceral, biological language for the Planet's suffering. Land near reactors is "getting thinner" (痩せていく, emaciated like a starving person). The planet's end is approaching ジワジワ — creepingly, inexorably, like a spreading stain. Red XIII's vow uses the plant-dying verb 枯れさせる. The wound in the Planet is 傷 (kizu, an open wound), not "scar." Barret talks about "the life of the Planet/star" (星の命), not just "the Planet."

The English repeatedly abstracts this into generic environmentalism or exhaustion idioms ("on its last legs," "sucked dry"). The Japanese makes the environmental case viscerally — you feel the Planet as a body being damaged. The English makes it intellectually — you understand the Planet is in trouble. For a game whose central thesis is that the Planet is alive, this is a significant weakening.

### Shinra as Bureaucracy vs. Shinra as Villainy

The Japanese Shinra commits evil through corporate process. Mass murder requires authorization from "the Shinra board" (collective decision), not "a Shinra Executive" (individual). The act of calling the Planet's life "Mako energy" is itself identified as the central deception — linguistic laundering. Rufus's chilling "failure is permitted once" (失敗は１度までだ) is corporate policy, stated as policy. The Confidential Report's most disturbing clause — "there is no problem in leaving him abandoned as-is" — is cut entirely from English.

Most subtly, a Midgar NPC uses the Japanese benefactive form (-てくれる) when describing what Shinra has "done for" the city — encoding ideological capture in grammar itself. You can't translate ideological capture performed through grammar into a language that doesn't have that grammar. But you could try. The English doesn't.

The result: Japanese Shinra is a recognizable corporation that does monstrous things through mundane procedures. English Shinra is a more conventionally evil organization. The Japanese is more politically pointed precisely because it's more banal.

### Vulnerability vs. Cool

This may be the single largest systematic shift. Across every character and every scene, the English localization:

- **Converts vulnerability to hostility or indifference.** Cloud's polite hedges become curt dismissals. Barret's moral reckonings become logistical complaints. Aeris's directness becomes hedging (which reads as shyness, a different kind of vulnerability, but the wrong one).

- **Completes unfinished sentences.** Japanese characters trail off, stutter, repeat themselves, leave thoughts grammatically broken. The emotional weight of *not being able to finish the sentence* — Barret's stutter before saying "pay" after Marlene gives him a flower; Gast's stammering his family's names; Vincent's mid-sentence self-correction — is consistently resolved into fluent English. The inarticulate becomes articulate, and something is lost every time.

- **Resolves ambiguity into clarity.** Cloud's breakdown speech leaves open whether his fear is genuinely his or Sephiroth's programming; the English resolves it. Aeris's "the first person I came to like" becomes "my first boyfriend," pre-answering Cloud's follow-up question and eliminating the bittersweet ambiguity about Zack.

- **Replaces somatic emotion with cognitive emotion.** "The back of my eyes is hot" (目の奥が熱い — the physical precursor to tears) becomes "my eyes are burning." "I somehow become literary" becomes "I get so mental." The body is replaced by the mind as the site of feeling.

The aggregate: the Japanese FF7 is a game about people who are bad at expressing their emotions, played in a language whose grammar lets you *hear* them failing. The English FF7 is a game about people who are *good* at suppressing their emotions, played in a language that makes them sound competent even when they're falling apart.

### Fate and Agency

Japanese characters accept fate more readily and explicitly. Cloud's rail metaphor specifies "rails that have been laid" (external force, passive voice). He describes himself as "being summoned" (passive) rather than chasing Sephiroth. Characters say しかたない ("it can't be helped") where the English gives active alternatives. Aeris uses the word 運命 (destiny/fate) where the English drops it.

The English tendency to convert passive acceptance into active choice sounds like an improvement, but it undermines the tragedy. When characters have more agency, their failures become their fault. When they're caught in systems and fate, their struggles are more sympathetic and their defiance more meaningful. The Japanese game earns its emotional weight partly through this fatalism — these people aren't heroes who chose wrong; they're people trying to act within constraints they can barely see.

## The Comedy Gap

Japanese FF7's humor is character-revealing: Mukki's grandiose invocation of "the grime and sweat of our youth" for a forced bath; the Honey Bee Inn soldiers whining in childish grammar about their armor; Jessie casting her bomb's first explosion as a "debut performance" (晴れぶたい); the vending machine's absurd 1990s health-product branding. The comedy tells you who people are.

English FF7's humor tends toward broader strokes: invented insults ("I've had better dog food"), American cultural references (a Gettysburg Address parody in Wutai), and randomness. It's often funny — sometimes funnier than the original — but it's less characterologically informative.

The biggest structural comedy loss is repetition-as-joke. The Chocobo Sage uses the exact same phrase every time his memory "returns" — the mechanical repetition *is* the joke. The English varies it each time, trying to avoid monotony, and kills the gag's architecture. Corneo's signature vocalization (ほひ～) maintains grotesque coherence across all his emotional states in Japanese; the English rotates approximations, undermining the characterization.

## The Error Layer

Beyond systematic shifts, approximately 30 lines across the 43 scenes say something materially different from or opposite to the Japanese:

- Reno's self-contradicting order ("Fire! — Don't fire!") becomes a straight command, removing both the comedy and the acknowledgment of Aeris's value to Shinra.
- Tifa's observation that humans can forget is reversed to "things they can never forget."
- Cloud's solidarity ("I'll fight here too") becomes isolation ("I'm going alone").
- Barret's "I can never fully atone" becomes "I can't keep up with this."
- Bugenhagen's self-critical "Am I accepting fate too readily?" becomes the opposite: "Am I wishing against fate?"
- Cloud's motive for saving Barret — the darkly comic "I'd have bad dreams" — is replaced wholesale with "We need your help to save the Planet," accidentally making Cloud noble where he was self-interestedly funny.
- Marlene's cheerful "um, listen!" (あのね！) becomes the distressed "Help, I'm—", implying she's being harmed when she's actually excited and happy.
- Ifalna's "Right now, I am very happy" becomes "I feel so much better now," converting a moment of pure joy into comparative recovery, making what follows less tragic.

These aren't systematic — they go in various directions — but they confirm that the translation was done under conditions where careful cross-checking wasn't possible.

## What the English Player Got

The English FF7 is a great game. Its rougher translation became part of its identity — "This guy are sick" and "Let's mosey" are beloved precisely because they're odd. Some English inventions are genuinely superior (the Rude/crude pun; "I've had better dog food" is funnier than the original's prim "it didn't suit my palate").

But the English player experienced a game that was emotionally cooler, thematically vaguer, and characterologically simpler than what the Japanese developers wrote. The environmental argument was more abstract. The corporate critique was more generic. The characters were tougher and less vulnerable. The humor was broader. The fate-vs-agency tension was muted. The grief was more punctuated and less sustained.

The deepest irony: FF7 is praised in the West for being the JRPG that "grew up" — the one that dealt with death, identity, and corporate evil in ways that felt adult. The Japanese original was even more adult than anyone knew. The translation didn't ruin the game — the story is too structurally strong for that — but it consistently filed down exactly the edges that made the original a more mature, more psychologically complex, more politically pointed work than the English-speaking world ever got to experience.
